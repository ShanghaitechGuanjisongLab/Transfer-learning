# Results Figure Outline

## Working title

**Engram-based cross-modal transfer depends on temporal continuity**

## Central narrative

Initial learning establishes a sparse motor-cortical memory ensemble. When a second cue–reward association is learned after a short interval, cells belonging to this ensemble are reactivated by the new sensory modality, their learned response structure remains readable, and their activity stabilizes early population responses. As the inter-task interval increases, ensemble reactivation and response-strength preservation weaken, cross-task decoding deteriorates, and both immediate transfer performance and subsequent acquisition become less efficient. Population heterogeneity, especially in L5, provides a complementary mechanism for improvement after transfer onset. An interval-sensitive recurrent network tests whether this cellular and population-level chain is sufficient to generate the observed learning dynamics.

The five figures should therefore form the following claim ladder:

**interval-dependent behavior → a transferable and necessary MOp ensemble → interval-dependent ensemble preservation → population dynamics linking preservation to learning → a minimal interval-sensitive circuit mechanism**

### Evidence-status labels used below

- **[E] Existing:** an analysis or result already represented in `Results.md` or current analysis scripts, although panel-specific implementation should still be checked.
- **[U] Unified reanalysis:** the method exists, but all interval groups must be recomputed with the same temporal anchors, cell criteria, and statistical model.
- **[N] New:** a new analysis or model condition is required; it must not be described as an established result before completion.

The proposed result statements below are conditional on the corresponding unified analyses supporting them.

|---|---|---|---|---|
| Short/interspersed | `ALInterspersed`, `LAInterspersed` | Audio→light and light→audio | Interspersed training; recover the actual interval from block/trial timestamps | Behavioral transfer, longitudinal ensemble reuse, single-cell response preservation, population analyses |

### Rules that should be fixed before making the main figures

1. **Do not call the 12-h group “no gap.”** Use `interspersed`, `12 h`, and `7 d` as the three principal conditions. Report measured inter-task intervals whenever timestamps permit.
2. **Use one anchor table for every neural analysis.** For each mouse, identify the first new-task block and the old-task block immediately preceding it. Do not use the globally last learned block when old-task trials continue after transfer onset in interspersed datasets.
3. **Do not session-average interspersed trials by `DateTime`.** Determine block order using `BlockUID`, `TrialIndex`, and the finest available timestamps. If block definitions are not comparable, analyze the first 30 new-task trials instead.
4. **Separate direction from interval.** The complete three-interval comparison is available for audio→light. Treat light→audio as a bidirectional replication for interspersed versus 12-h conditions; do not imply a light→audio 7-d group.
5. **Keep the mouse as the inferential unit.** Report cell numbers descriptively. Use per-mouse values or mixed-effects models with mouse-level grouping rather than treating cells as independent replicates.
6. **Separate the two behavioral components.** Analyze first-block/first-30-trial performance separately from sigmoid slope and trials/blocks to criterion. Because block spacing differs among paradigms, block slope is not automatically a learning rate in absolute clock time.
7. **Freeze the operational ensemble definition.** At cue +1 s, define learned-active cells using the established baseline rule (baseline −3 to 0 s; activity above baseline mean +3 SD). Call this the `learned-active ensemble` or `putative engram ensemble`; the stronger engram interpretation rests on the conjunction of learning-dependent recruitment, cross-modal reactivation, outcome relation, and cFos-TRAP necessity.
8. **Distinguish overlap magnitude from overlap existence.** In addition to raw reactivation, report chance-corrected overlap and total new-task active-cell fractions. This prevents changes in overall activity or registration yield from being mistaken for specific engram loss.
9. **Harmonize population metrics before comparing intervals.** Use the same z-scoring, missing-trial exclusion, zero-anchoring choice, layer-weighting rule, and $|z|\leq1$ criterion for every cohort. Existing divergence scripts do not all use identical preprocessing.
10. **Do not overstate the temporal design.** These are cohort-level interval comparisons, not continuous within-animal measurements of decay during the gap. Three conditions support an ordered interval effect if observed, but not by themselves an exponential decay, a precise threshold, or complete erasure.
11. **Report interval-associated procedural differences.** The 7-d condition also includes home-cage housing, restored water access, and renewed water restriction. It is an interval manipulation, but not a manipulation of elapsed time alone.

# Figure 1 | Temporal continuity determines the efficiency of cross-modal transfer

### Question

Does the interval between initial and new-task learning determine how quickly the new association is acquired?

### Data

- `ALInterspersed` and `LAInterspersed`: interspersed/short-continuity condition.
- `AudioLightBaseline` and `LightAudioBaseline`: nominal 12-h condition.
- `Vacation7`: 7-d condition, audio→light only.
- Direction-matched naïve learning blocks as reference curves where required.
- Existing delayed and pure-behavior datasets should remain controls or supplements, not replace the core undelayed interval datasets.

### Proposed panels and analyses

**A. Experimental design.** Schematics of initial task A, the inter-task interval, and new task B for interspersed, 12-h, and 7-d conditions; show both transfer directions and mark that the 7-d condition is audio→light only. Include longitudinal MOp L2/3/L5 imaging and the common cue +1 s analysis time. **[E/U]**

**B. Interval verification.** Plot the measured interval between the end of the anchored old-task block and the start of the first new-task block for each mouse. Use actual hours on a log-scaled axis if within-session timestamps are available; otherwise keep the short condition explicitly categorical as `interspersed`, without assigning it an unsupported numerical duration. **[N]**

**C. New-task learning curves.** Plot hit rate against new-task block and, in a matched companion view, against cumulative new-task trials. Show the full three-interval audio→light comparison; show light→audio separately as a two-interval replication. Use a mixed-effects model with interval, block, and direction where appropriate, followed by prespecified interval contrasts. **[E: 12 h versus 7 d; U: all groups]**

**D. Immediate transfer.** Quantify first-block or first-30-new-trial hit rate per mouse. Test an ordered interval effect in audio→light data and report each group without assuming monotonicity in advance. Compare with direction-matched naïve performance only as a secondary reference. **[E: 12 h versus 7 d; U: all groups]**

**E. Subsequent acquisition.** Show per-mouse sigmoid slope and trials/blocks to criterion separately from first-block performance. Test whether interval affects only the starting point or also subsequent improvement. If interspersed training prevents a comparable sigmoid fit, use a trial-based acquisition measure rather than forcing the same fit. **[E: existing slope methods; U/N: unified interval comparison]**

**F. Behavioral controls.** Report terminal old-task performance, final asymptotic new-task performance, and basic lick/motivation measures. These controls test whether an interval effect on transfer is secondary to poorer original learning or a global inability to perform. Move detailed versions to Supplementary Figure S1 if space is limited. **[U]**

### Intended sub-conclusion

Cross-modal transfer is strongest when initial and new-task learning are temporally continuous and becomes less efficient after longer intervals. Immediate performance and subsequent acquisition should be reported as distinct behavioral endpoints rather than compressed into one learning-curve effect.

### Connection to the next figure

Figure 1 establishes the behavioral phenomenon but not its cellular basis. Figure 2 therefore asks what initial learning leaves behind in MOp, whether that ensemble is reused across sensory modalities, and whether it is required for efficient transfer.

### Relation to the title

This figure supplies the behavioral evidence for **“depends on temporal continuity.”** It does not, by itself, establish the engram mechanism.

### Literature-derived organization

- **Cai et al. (2016), Fig. 3f,g and Extended Data Fig. 4:** establish strengthening of the second memory across temporal intervals and provide a time-course organization before causal rescue experiments. Figure 1 borrows this behavior-first, interval-as-independent-variable structure.
- **Rashid et al. (2016), Fig. 1B:** compare several inter-training intervals and show enhancement of event-2 memory at short but not long intervals. This motivates presenting second-task enhancement as the primary behavioral endpoint and avoiding a simple old-memory-strength interpretation.
- These studies guide logic only; their hippocampal/amygdalar time windows must not be assumed to be identical in MOp.

# Figure 2 | Initial learning establishes a cross-modally reusable MOp ensemble required for transfer

### Question

What is transferred at the cellular level: a sensory-cue representation, or a learned outcome-related ensemble that can be engaged by another modality?

### Data

- Reference neural analyses: `AudioLightBaseline`, with `LightAudioBaseline` used for reverse-direction replication.
- Naïve/calibration, learned old-task, and first new-task blocks from the same registered cells.
- Existing cFos-TRAP MOp silencing and control cohorts for causal necessity.
- The ensemble definitions developed here are then applied separately within every interval group in Figure 3.

### Proposed panels and analyses

**A. Longitudinal identification of the learned ensemble.** Show registered MOp cells across naïve cue exposure, learned task A, and first task B. Define learned-active cells at cue +1 s and display representative single-cell traces plus an old-task/new-task heatmap pair with a common cell order. **[E/U]**

**B. Single-cell transfer dynamics.** Classify each registered cell by its activity state in the two tasks: maintained/learned-active and reactivated, learned-active but not reactivated, newly recruited in task B, and remaining inactive; retain response sign so that excited and suppressed cells are not collapsed. Show a state-transition diagram and per-cell response changes. This explicitly captures maintenance, loss, recruitment, and outcome-selective stabilization rather than describing transfer as a uniform amplification. **[E: reuse and hit/miss components; U: joint transition analysis]**

**C. Specific ensemble reuse.** Quantify per-mouse reactivation probability, $P(B\text{-active}\mid A\text{-learned-active})$, and compare it with chance determined by the overall fraction of B-active registered cells. Show hit-versus-miss reactivation where trial counts permit. Report the absolute numbers of learned-active, B-active, and shared cells alongside the proportions. **[E: audio→light; U: reverse replication and common layer weighting]**

**D. Identity of the transferred code.** Apply the established cue decoder and hit/miss behavior decoder without retraining to the held-out new-modality block. Use within-context cross-validation as a decoder-quality control and label-shuffled nulls for transfer. The key test is whether outcome-related information generalizes even when audio-versus-light identity does not. **[E: existing Results analysis; U: consolidate the decoder implementation and use identical balancing/null procedures]**

**E. Outcome-selective cellular substrate.** Show hit-preferring and miss-preferring decoder weights and compare their inter-trial stability on matched outcome trials. Keep this panel only if it adds evidence beyond C and D; otherwise move decoder-weight composition to the supplement. **[E/U]**

**F. Necessity of the tagged ensemble.** Illustrate cFos-TRAP tagging during the last old-task training block and silencing during new-task learning, followed by tagging histology and the new-task learning curve. Report the supported overall learning effect; do not claim a selective first-block deficit because the existing cFos first-block comparison was not significant. Silencing establishes necessity, not sufficiency. **[E]**

### Intended sub-conclusion

Initial associative learning produces a sparse, outcome-related MOp ensemble whose cells exhibit distinct maintenance, loss, recruitment, and stabilization dynamics when the cue modality changes. Cross-modal reactivation of this ensemble is behaviorally relevant, and silencing the activity-tagged ensemble impairs efficient transfer learning.

### Connection from Figure 1 and to Figure 3

Figure 1 shows that transfer changes with interval; Figure 2 identifies the candidate cellular vehicle at the reference interval. Figure 3 then directly tests whether the probability, strength, and readability of this vehicle are preserved across short, intermediate, and long intervals.

### Relation to the title

This figure establishes **“engram-based cross-modal transfer.”** Calcium reuse alone is treated as ensemble evidence; the activity-dependent tagging and necessity experiment is what most strongly supports the engram interpretation.

### Literature-derived organization

- **Chen et al. (2026), Fig. 1c–i:** first identify a functionally defined M1 ensemble, then test its targeted engagement and measure recruitment of the remaining population. Figure 2 follows this identify-ensemble → test functional relevance → examine population readout sequence.
- **Chen et al. (2026), Fig. 2:** early- versus late-onset target comparisons demonstrate the value of specificity controls. The cue-versus-outcome decoder contrast and active-fraction/chance controls serve an analogous conceptual role here, although the experiments differ.
- **Cai et al. (2016), Figs. 1 and 2:** use longitudinal calcium overlap and an independent tagging method as convergent evidence for shared ensembles. This supports combining registered-cell reuse with cFos-TRAP rather than relying on one operational definition.
- **Choucry et al. (2024), Introduction and Fig. 1:** engram claims are organized around learning-related activation, reactivation, necessity, and sufficiency. The present outline claims necessity only because no ensemble-activation sufficiency experiment is included.

# Figure 3 | Temporal continuity preserves engram-cell activity and its cross-task readout

### Question

Does the learned MOp ensemble remain available to the new task in an interval-dependent manner, both in terms of which cells respond and how strongly their learned responses are preserved?

### Data

- All three interval conditions: `ALInterspersed`/`LAInterspersed`, `AudioLightBaseline`/`LightAudioBaseline`, and `Vacation7`.
- Longitudinally matched cells from the anchored learned old-task block and first new-task block.
- The same learned-active definition, layer rules, and quality criteria as Figure 2.

### Proposed panels and analyses

**A. Ensemble preservation across intervals.** For each interval, show learned-task and first-new-task heatmaps of the same registered cells, sorted only by the learned old-task response. Include a representative single-cell trace for maintained, attenuated, and newly recruited response patterns. Use descriptive group heatmaps only as companions to per-mouse statistics. **[E: 12 h versus 7 d; U: all groups]**

**B. Interval dependence of ensemble reuse.** Plot per-mouse reactivation probability against interval, separately by layer and with a prespecified learned-active-count-weighted combined measure. Add chance-corrected excess overlap, for example $O_{\mathrm{excess}}=(P_{\mathrm{obs}}-P_{\mathrm{chance}})/(1-P_{\mathrm{chance}})$, where $P_{\mathrm{chance}}$ is the new-task active fraction among matched cells. Test the ordered interval effect and show both transfer directions where available. **[E: 12 h versus 7 d raw reuse; U/N: unified and chance-corrected comparison]**

**C. Preservation of response strength.** Within learned-active cells, compare trial-mean z-scored activity at cue +1 s between old and new tasks. Use per-mouse paired amplitude differences and an old-to-new regression slope/correlation as the primary preservation measures; avoid relying solely on a binary active/inactive threshold. A secondary amplitude-retention ratio may be shown only for cells with robust learned responses. This panel directly tests maintenance of engram-cell activity intensity. **[N/U]**

**D. Persistence of the learned population code.** Train the established pre-cue versus post-cue LASSO logistic decoder on learned old-task trials and apply it without retraining to the first new-task block. Show within-task cross-validation, new-task readout over time, the label-shuffled null, and per-mouse readout at cue +1 s across intervals. This tests whether the preserved response remains functionally readable, not merely whether some cells overlap. **[E: 12 h versus 7 d; U: interspersed and reverse direction]**

**E. Neural preservation predicts immediate behavior.** Across mice, relate reactivation probability, response-strength preservation, and decoder readout to first-block/first-30-trial performance. Use one prespecified primary neural predictor and treat the others as convergent secondary measures; include direction as a covariate where appropriate. Any mediation analysis should be explicitly exploratory and must not be presented as proof of a causal chain. **[E: divergence/reactivation-behavior associations; U/N: unified preservation-behavior analysis]**

**F. Specificity controls.** Show total old- and new-task active fractions, registered-cell yield, and, if available, same-cue reactivation or registration-shuffle controls. These distinguish selective loss of the learned ensemble from global activity suppression or poorer longitudinal registration after longer intervals. **[U/N; detailed versions may be supplementary]**

### Intended sub-conclusion

Temporal continuity preserves both the recruitment probability and response strength of the learned MOp ensemble, maintaining a population code that the new task can read out immediately. Longer intervals reduce this preservation and its relation to immediate transfer performance. Use `interval-dependent preservation` rather than claiming that the same animals' engrams were continuously observed to decay.

### Connection from Figure 2 and to Figure 4

Figure 2 defines the transferable ensemble; Figure 3 makes its preservation the central interval-dependent cellular result. Figure 4 next asks how this preserved cellular substrate changes whole-population trial stability and subsequent learning-related heterogeneity.

### Relation to the title

This is the central mechanistic figure for **“depends on temporal continuity”**: it links the interval manipulation to maintenance of engram-cell activity, rather than only to behavior.

### Literature-derived organization

- **Cai et al. (2016), Figs. 1f and 2f:** compare ensemble overlap at 5 h versus 7 d and compare observed overlap with chance. Figure 3 adopts the same requirement that reuse exceed a baseline defined by overall activity.
- **Cai et al. (2016), Extended Data Fig. 4:** orders ensemble-overlap and behavioral-linking measures across 5 h, 2 d, and 7 d. This motivates putting cellular preservation and behavioral readout in the same interval-centered figure.
- **Choucry et al. (2024), “Engram overlap in memory linking—Evidence for engram overlap” and Fig. 2b:** emphasize that the magnitude of overlap, rather than its mere presence, determines memory linking. This directly motivates the response-strength and chance-corrected analyses in B and C.
- **Chen et al. (2026), Fig. 3d:** use a decoder trained in one condition and tested in another to demonstrate shared population readout. Figure 3D borrows this cross-context decoder logic, with a label-shuffled null and no retraining.
- **Rashid et al. (2016), Fig. 1C:** connects short- versus long-interval co-allocation with memory interaction and provides a conceptual precedent for interval-dependent ensemble sharing.

# Figure 4 | Preserved ensembles and heterogeneous population dynamics support distinct stages of transfer learning

### Question

How does interval-dependent ensemble preservation affect population activity, and are immediate transfer and subsequent learning speed supported by the same neural property?

### Data

- First new-task blocks from all core interval datasets for PCA and inter-trial divergence.
- Pre-ceiling new-task sessions from the same datasets for learning-related response heterogeneity, with first-block heterogeneity analyzed separately if used for interval comparisons.
- Naïve reference blocks from the established cohorts.
- `THInhibit` and its established control cohort for the orthogonal thalamic manipulation.

### Proposed panels and analyses

**A. Early population trajectories.** Project first-new-task population responses into a common within-mouse PC space and visualize trial-ordered trajectories for each interval. Keep layer-specific analyses, especially the established L2/3 effect, and avoid fitting one pooled PCA across unrelated mice. **[E: naïve/12-h trajectories; U: interval conditions]**

**B. Inter-trial divergence and immediate performance.** Compute the established normalized trial-to-trial divergence at cue +1 s for each mouse and layer, then compare intervals and relate divergence to first-block behavior. Use exactly the same zero-anchoring and complete-trial cell rules in every cohort. Test whether reactivation and divergence provide complementary or redundant prediction of immediate performance. **[E: naïve/12 h and 12 h/7 d comparisons; U: all groups and harmonized implementation]**

**C. Response heterogeneity across intervals.** Show per-cell response distributions and response heterogeneity (across-cell SD of trial-mean z-scored responses, with the established moderate-response cell criterion) by layer. Use first-block RH only for first-block questions; use pre-ceiling RH for learning-slope questions, and label these estimands distinctly. **[E: 12-h RH; U/N: unified interval comparison]**

**D. Heterogeneity predicts subsequent learning.** Relate per-mouse L5 and L2/3 RH to sigmoid learning slope or trial-based acquisition rate. Test whether L5 RH explains subsequent improvement after accounting for initial performance and the primary ensemble-preservation measure from Figure 3. Avoid interpreting a residual correlation as mediation unless a formal, appropriately powered analysis supports it. **[E: RH-slope association; U: interval-aware model]**

**E. Thalamic support for the heterogeneity–learning relationship.** Show the established TH-inhibition learning curves, first-block comparison, and layer-specific RH. This manipulation tests whether thalamic input supports L5 heterogeneity and subsequent learning speed while leaving immediate performance comparatively intact. It is an indirect causal test of the heterogeneity pathway, not direct proof that RH itself is causal and not an interval manipulation. **[E]**

### Intended sub-conclusion

Preserved ensemble activity is associated with stable early population responses and strong immediate performance, whereas L5 response heterogeneity and its thalamic support are associated primarily with subsequent improvement. The two pathways are complementary: interval-dependent engram preservation should not be used as a blanket explanation for every aspect of learning speed unless the unified analyses support that conclusion.

### Connection from Figure 3 and to Figure 5

Figure 3 identifies which cellular features are preserved; Figure 4 explains their population-level consequences and separates immediate from subsequent learning. Figure 5 then tests whether a recurrent circuit containing ensemble retention, plastic inhibition, and a taught readout can generate this full pattern.

### Relation to the title

This figure connects **temporally maintained engram activity** to the neural dynamics that make **cross-modal transfer** fast, while preventing the manuscript from reducing all transfer dynamics to a single overlap metric.

### Literature-derived organization

- **Chen et al. (2026), Fig. 3a–c:** relate population-activity distances to trial-by-trial behavioral similarity before introducing decoding. Figure 4 follows this pattern of connecting population geometry to behavioral variability.
- **Chen et al. (2026), Fig. 4:** show that the initial population state predicts subsequent activity and movement. This motivates separating the initial state/early stability from later learning-related heterogeneity.
- **Perez-Nieves et al. (2021):** provide the computational rationale for heterogeneity as a facilitator of robust learning. The present figure tests this as a complement to, not a replacement for, ensemble preservation.
- **Rashid et al. (2016), Fig. 4 and Choucry et al. (2024), mechanisms sections:** motivate considering excitation–inhibition organization downstream of ensemble allocation; the TH/RNN results address circuit organization rather than merely counting shared cells.

# Figure 5 | An interval-sensitive recurrent model links engram retention to transfer-learning dynamics

### Question

Can a minimal recurrent circuit in which learned ensemble structure persists to different degrees generate the observed interval-dependent behavior, single-cell reuse, and population dynamics?

### Data and model status

- Existing RNN conditions: naïve, transfer, and reduced teaching signal (`THOff`).
- Existing model readouts: learning curves, first-formal-training responses, layer/cell-type RH, and pre-formal synaptic-weight distributions.
- **Required extension:** an explicit between-task retention manipulation. The existing RNN has no interspersed, 12-h, or 7-d condition and therefore cannot currently be cited as evidence for temporal continuity.

### Proposed panels and analyses

**A. Circuit and interval manipulation.** Show cue-driven L2/3 and L5 excitatory populations, local plastic inhibitory pools, and the taught behavioral readout. Insert a between-task interval that changes the accessibility of the learned ensemble through a defined retention mechanism, such as decay of learning-induced excitability and/or selected task-A-potentiated recurrent structure. Do not rescale task-B performance directly. **[E: circuit; N: interval mechanism]**

**B. Neural calibration before behavioral prediction.** Choose retention levels using measured ensemble-reactivation and response-strength preservation from Figure 3, without fitting first-block hit rate or learning slope. Show simulated learned-cell reuse, activity-strength preservation, and cross-task response similarity for high, intermediate, and low retention. If an explicit time constant is used, its mapping to real hours must be independently justified; otherwise label conditions as ordinal retention regimes. **[N]**

**C. Emergent interval-dependent transfer.** Simulate task-B learning for naïve/no-pretraining and high-, intermediate-, and low-retention transfer conditions. Measure first-block performance, sigmoid slope/trials to criterion, reactivation, and divergence using readouts analogous to the empirical analyses. Test whether reduced retention reproduces weaker immediate transfer and slower acquisition without being imposed directly on behavior. **[E: naïve versus transfer learning; N: interval conditions and matched readouts]**

**D. Single-cell and population mechanisms.** Show maintained, lost, and newly recruited model cells and the evolution of L5 excitatory RH and recurrent-weight distributions. Compare these patterns qualitatively and, where scaling permits, quantitatively with Figures 2–4. Retain the existing THOff condition as a separate reduction of the teaching signal. **[E: existing RH/weights/THOff; U/N: interval-resolved model analysis]**

**E. Perturbation dissociation.** Compare reduced ensemble retention with reduced thalamic teaching input. The model prediction to test is that these perturbations preferentially affect immediate ensemble-dependent transfer and subsequent heterogeneity-dependent acquisition, respectively. Treat this as a model-generated dissociation unless supported by a direct in-vivo interaction experiment. **[E: THOff; N: retention×teaching comparison]**

### Intended sub-conclusion

A recurrent circuit with plastic inhibition can be sufficient in silico to transform interval-dependent preservation of a learned ensemble into cross-modal transfer and its characteristic population dynamics. The model provides mechanistic sufficiency and testable predictions; it does not replace the in-vivo necessity experiments or prove the biological retention mechanism.

### Connection to the overall conclusion

Figures 1–4 establish the behavioral phenomenon, cellular substrate, interval dependence, and population correlates. Figure 5 closes the loop by asking whether these elements are jointly sufficient in a minimal circuit and by generating perturbations that can be tested experimentally.

### Relation to the title

The model integrates the complete proposed chain: **temporal continuity → engram accessibility/activity preservation → stable and heterogeneous population dynamics → efficient cross-modal transfer**.

### Literature-derived organization

- **Chen et al. (2026), Discussion:** proposes recurrent pattern completion, appropriate initial states, and specific triggering inputs as jointly necessary for learned M1 activity. Figure 5 adopts this separation between stored recurrent structure, initial state, and new-cue input.
- **Rashid et al. (2016), Fig. 2B and Figs. 3–4:** first formalize transient excitability-based ensemble allocation and then use bidirectional perturbations. This motivates modeling retention as a neural-state variable and testing distinct perturbations rather than hard-coding behavioral decay.
- **Choucry et al. (2024), “Somato-synaptic model for memory linking,” Fig. 3:** provide a precedent for a model that preserves both shared and task-specific features. The RNN should therefore examine newly recruited/task-specific cells as well as shared cells, avoiding the implication that all task-B cells must belong to the old ensemble.
- **Perez-Nieves et al. (2021):** motivate retaining neuronal/response heterogeneity as a learning mechanism within the model rather than treating successful transfer as identical activation of every cell.

## Cross-figure evidentiary checks

| Claim required by the title | Primary figure | Minimum acceptable evidence | Overclaim to avoid |
|---|---|---|---|
| Transfer depends on temporal continuity | Figure 1 | Interval-dependent first-task-B performance and/or acquisition with appropriate direction and cohort controls | Calling 12 h `no gap`; claiming a precise decay law from three conditions |
| MOp transfer is engram-based | Figure 2 | Learning-dependent ensemble, cross-modal reactivation, outcome relation, and activity-tagged necessity | Treating calcium overlap alone as a complete engram demonstration; claiming sufficiency from silencing |
| Engram activity is maintained according to interval | Figure 3 | Per-mouse reuse plus response-strength preservation and cross-task readout across intervals | Inferring within-animal decay during an unimaged gap; relying only on binary overlap |
| Maintained ensembles produce useful transfer dynamics | Figure 4 | Harmonized divergence/RH analyses linked to distinct behavioral components | Assuming RH is directly causal from TH inhibition alone; mixing first-block and pre-ceiling RH |
| A circuit mechanism can account for the full pattern | Figure 5 | Explicit interval-sensitive model with neurally calibrated retention and emergent behavior | Using the existing no-interval RNN as evidence for temporal continuity |

## Recommended supplementary controls

1. **S1 — Paradigm and cohort controls:** delayed versus undelayed behavior, full cohort flowcharts, per-panel mouse/cell counts, and direction-specific analyses.
2. **S2 — Interval and behavioral controls:** exact interval distributions, terminal old-task performance, motivation/licking, and acquisition plotted by both trials and blocks.
3. **S3 — Reuse robustness:** alternative activity thresholds, chance-corrected overlap, total active fractions, registration shuffles, and layer-specific results.
4. **S4 — Decoder robustness:** within-context cross-validation, class balancing, label-shuffled nulls, alternative training windows, and exclusions.
5. **S5 — Population-metric robustness:** divergence with and without zero-anchoring, missing-cell rules, RH cell-selection thresholds, and block-versus-trial learning measures.
6. **S6 — Manipulation controls:** cFos tagging and TH effector histology, control-virus/drug groups, and the non-significant cFos first-block result where relevant.
