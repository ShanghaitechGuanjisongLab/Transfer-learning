# Results
## Transfer learning improves initial performance through reactivation of pre-training ensembles
We asked whether previous learning accelerated acquisition of a new cue modality while preserving the same licking response. Mice first learned an auditory-cued water task and were then transferred to a visual-cued water task; the overall design is summarized in Figure 1A. In the visual task, transfer mice showed a higher first-block hit rate and an upward-shifted learning curve relative to naive mice (Figure 1B). Even when naive and transfer mice were matched for initial performance, transfer mice still reached criterion earlier, and their residualized learning slopes remained larger after adjustment for initial hit rate (Figure 1C). We therefore separated the transfer advantage into an initial-performance component and a subsequent-learning component. We next targeted MOp for longitudinal two-photon imaging on the basis of its position in the cue-to-lick sensorimotor pathway (Figure 1D,E).

We then tested whether the elevated initial performance during transfer reflected reuse of ensembles recruited during pre-training. Cells active at 1 s in the learned auditory task were frequently reactivated during transfer to the visual task, as illustrated by the heatmaps, a representative reused cell, and the corresponding population-average traces (Figure 1F-H). At the population level, a substantial fraction of cells active during learned auditory performance remained active during visual transfer (Figure 1I). Reactivation was higher in hit than in miss trials (Figure 1J), and mice with higher reactivation rates showed higher initial transfer performance (Figure 1K). These results indicate that the early behavioral advantage of transfer is associated with reuse of pre-training task-related ensembles rather than with a nonspecific increase in activity.

## Inherited ensembles reduce inter-trial divergence and are required for cross-modal transfer
Transfer did not simply increase overall response amplitude. Global heatmaps and population-average traces overlapped strongly between naive and transfer visual learning (Figure S1A,B), whereas the 1 s z-score and the fraction of active cells changed in opposite directions (Figure S1C). Together with the schematic summary in Figure S1D, this pattern argues for redistribution of population responses rather than uniform gain amplification. This interpretation is consistent with prior evidence that motor-cortical learning reorganizes population structure more than it globally elevates activity (Komiyama et al., 2010).

We therefore quantified trial-to-trial stability. Representative single cells showed more reproducible responses after learning and during transfer than during naive training (Figure 2A), and the same ordered cell set revealed more consistent 0-1.5 s activity in the learned and transfer-hit conditions than in the transfer-miss condition (Figure 2B). PCA trajectories further showed that learned activity occupied a more convergent region of state space than naive activity (Figure 2C). Relative divergence was reduced after learning and was lower in inherited than in non-inherited cell subgroups during transfer (Figure 2D). Pseudo-simultaneous imaging of MOp layers 2/3 and 5 confirmed layer-specific effects (Figure 2E): divergence decreased after learning in the auditory task without reaching significance in either layer (Figure 2F), but across mice the first-block hit rate was related more strongly to layer-5 divergence than to layer-2/3 divergence (Figure 2G). During visual learning, transfer selectively reduced divergence in layer 2/3, whereas layer 5 showed no clear group difference (Figure 2H). This state-space framing is consistent with prior work showing that stable behavior can persist despite single-neuron variability when latent population dynamics remain conserved (Gallego et al., 2020).

We next tested whether the reused ensembles were required for transfer. In cFos-CreER mice, cells active during the learned auditory task were tagged and then chemogenetically inhibited during visual transfer (Figure 2I,J). This manipulation shifted the transfer learning curve downward (Figure 2K), indicating that the tagged ensemble contributed causally to successful transfer. As an orthogonal manipulation, we inserted a 7-day interval between auditory learning and visual transfer (Figure 2L). The interval reduced both initial performance and overall transfer progress (Figure 2M), and it also lowered reactivation while increasing divergence (Figure 2N). The summary model in Figure 2O captures these results: reused cells preserve a learned-like population state, suppress inter-trial divergence, and support successful cross-modal transfer.

## Inherited response heterogeneity supports faster subsequent learning under thalamic modulation
The initial advantage did not fully explain why transfer mice continued to improve faster. We therefore quantified day-to-day performance increments as Delta Hit (Figure 3A) and defined response heterogeneity as the across-cell standard deviation of trial-averaged 1 s z-scores after restricting the analysis to moderately responding cells in the range [-1, 1] (Figure 3B). State-space analyses supported a geometric difference between de novo and transfer learning: representative cells and trajectories showed more monotonic progression during transfer (Figure S2A-C), the path-to-distance ratio was lower in layer 5 (Figure S2D), projection onto the start-to-end direction was larger in layer 2/3 (Figure S2F), and the mean step length itself did not increase (Figure S2E). Thus, faster subsequent learning reflected more directed progress through state space rather than larger nonspecific updates per training day. This geometry is consistent with low-dimensional preparatory dynamics reported in motor cortex (Inagaki et al., 2018).

Response heterogeneity captured a complementary cellular signature of this effect. Across mice, heterogeneity correlated with learning slope in layer 2/3 but not in layer 5 (Figure 3C). At the group level, heterogeneity showed a different layer dependence: the naive-to-transfer difference was evident in layer 5, whereas layer 2/3 was more clearly separated between the learned auditory and transfer states (Figure 3D). Representative naive and transfer sessions showed that transfer shifted the distribution of cellular responses away from zero and toward stronger positive and negative polarization (Figure 3E). Together, these results indicate that the across-mouse correlate of subsequent learning was strongest in layer 2/3, whereas the group-level shift between de novo and transfer learning was expressed more strongly in layer 5. Because deep-layer output neurons are themselves heterogeneous and functionally specialized, this interpretation is compatible with prior evidence for diversified layer-5 motor-cortical subpopulations (Oswald et al., 2013; Currie et al., 2022).

To identify an upstream factor that supports this architecture, we inhibited posterior thalamus centered on PO throughout transfer learning (Figure 3F and Figure S3). Thalamic inhibition had little effect on initial performance (Figure 3G), but representative sessions showed weaker polarization of the 1 s response distribution under inhibition (Figure 3H). Correlations between heterogeneity and learning slope remained strongest in layer 2/3 (Figure 3I), whereas thalamic inhibition reduced both Delta Hit and layer-5 heterogeneity at the group level (Figure 3J). Figure 3K summarizes this dissociation: prior-structure recruitment supports the initial advantage, whereas thalamus-dependent response heterogeneity supports the speed of subsequent learning. Consistent with this separation, reactivation rate and divergence, which explained initial performance, did not significantly explain Delta Hit in the negative-control analysis (Figure 3L).

## RSPd provides visually biased upstream input but only modestly affects transfer learning
We next examined RSPd as a candidate visually biased upstream region. Its position between visual cortex and motor cortex is illustrated in Figure 4A. Population heatmaps showed that RSPd responded more strongly to the visual task than to the auditory task, with relatively modest separation between transfer-hit and transfer-miss trials (Figure 4B). The visual response in RSPd also rose earlier than the corresponding response in MOp (Figure 4C), consistent with a more upstream visual role. Across mice, however, reactivation of auditory-task-active RSPd cells did not significantly predict initial transfer performance (Figure 4D). Reactivation was only modestly higher in hit than in miss trials (Figure 4E), and the average calcium traces differed little across learned, transfer-hit, and transfer-miss conditions (Figure 4F). This pattern matches previous work placing RSP at the intersection of visually guided and contextual processing rather than at the final motor bottleneck (Alexander et al., 2023; Vedder et al., 2017).

Chemogenetic inhibition supported this interpretation. The RSPd manipulation and its viral expression control are shown in Figure 4G and Figure S4. Inhibiting RSPd alone did not clearly impair transfer learning, whereas combined inhibition of RSPd and MOp produced a larger bottleneck during progression toward criterion (Figure 4H). RSPd therefore appears to contribute visually biased upstream input to the broader sensorimotor network, but it is not the primary locus that determines transfer success.
