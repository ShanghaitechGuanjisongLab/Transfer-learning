# Inter-trial stability and inter-cell heterogeneity of the cortical response of the transfer task

To determine how prior auditory learning shapes cortical processing of a novel visual cue, mice were trained on a cross-modal transfer paradigm: after learning an audio–water association task, the cue modality was switched to light while the reward rule remained unchanged (Figure 1A). Mice with auditory pre-training (transfer) attained a higher hit rate than naïve mice trained directly on the light cue across the first seven light-water blocks (Figure 1B). To follow the underlying cortical representations, we performed two-photon calcium imaging in layer 2/3 and layer 5 of the primary motor cortex (MOp) across auditory training and the subsequent light training (Figure 1C). In naïve mice, single cells responded to the first light cue with large and trial-variable calcium transients, whereas the same cells recorded after auditory learning responded more consistently from trial to trial, and this trial-to-trial consistency was already present in the first light-water block (Figure 1D).

We first quantified trial-to-trial stability at cue +1 s — the moment at which water is delivered, and thus the latest time point at which cortical activity can still influence the reward outcome of the trial — using an inter-trial divergence index, defined as the trial-to-trial variance of the population response relative to its squared mean, computed separately for each layer and each mouse. In L2/3, transfer mice showed markedly lower divergence than naïve mice upon their first light-water block, whereas in L5 the two groups did not differ (Figure 1E). Across the 25 mice of both cohorts, inter-trial divergence correlated strongly and negatively with first-block hit rate (Figure 1F), indicating that mice whose cortical population responses were more stable at cue +1 s performed better at the very first exposure to the light cue. Divergence, however, did not predict how fast mice subsequently learned (Figure 1G). Inter-trial stability therefore accounts for the starting point of transfer, but not for the speed of new learning, prompting us to search for a population property that tracks learning speed.

We next measured response heterogeneity (RH), the across-cell standard deviation of trial-mean z-scored responses at cue +1 s, as an index of functional differentiation within the recorded population. In learning theory, a spread of response profiles across network units provides the differentiated updates that gradient descent requires, and thereby accelerates convergence (Saxe et al., 2014). In biological networks, heterogeneity plays a comparable enabling role, promoting robust learning (Perez-Nieves et al., 2021). Intuitively, RH reflects how strongly the recorded cells are polarized into cue-excited and cue-suppressed subpopulations: a single transfer block shows a wider spread of per-cell response amplitudes than a naïve block (Figure 1J). Quantitatively, RH correlated significantly with the sigmoid learning slope in L5 but not in L2/3 (Figure 1H): mice with more heterogeneous L5 responses learned the light task faster. Consistently, transfer mice exhibited significantly higher RH than naïve mice in L5, whereas the L2/3 difference did not reach significance; fully learned audio-water blocks showed the highest RH in both layers (Figure 1I). Thus, inter-trial stability governs immediate transfer performance, while inter-cell heterogeneity, particularly in L5, governs the speed of subsequent learning.

Two sets of control observations support this framework (Figure S1). First, the behavioral quantification in Figure 1B used the delayed paradigm, in line with the conventional design of such transfer tasks, whereas all imaging analyses above were performed in the undelayed paradigm, in which water is delivered at a fixed time (800 ms after cue offset) on every trial regardless of licking; this response-independent reward timing gives the external task events a trial-invariant temporal anchor, which is required for aligning calcium signals across trials. To confirm that the behavioral advantage does not depend on this paradigm difference, we re-examined learning in the undelayed variant: transfer mice still outperformed naïve mice from the very first block and reached asymptotic performance in fewer blocks (Figure S1A–D). Second, during transfer, the ensemble that had been active in learned audio-water blocks was re-engaged by the light cue: among cells active at cue +1 s in learned audio-water blocks, the reactivation fraction was higher on hit than on miss trials (Figure S1E–G), and roughly one seventh of them (76/511) were active again in the first transfer light-water block (Figure S1H). The ensemble carrying the auditory memory is therefore recruited by the novel visual cue, and the degree of this recruitment covaries with trial outcome.

# Encoding ensemble facilitates first-block performance

The first-block transfer advantage implies that auditory learning leaves a cortical representation that the light task can read out immediately. To identify what is transferred, we trained two linear decoders on MOp population activity: a cue decoder that discriminates auditory from light stimuli on calibration blocks, and a behavior decoder that discriminates hit from miss trials on audio-water training blocks; both were then applied, without retraining, to the held-out transfer light-water block (Figure 2A). In their training contexts both decoders tracked their labels (Figure 2A, left). In the transfer light-water block, however, only the behavior decoder separated light hit from light miss trials, whereas the cue decoder remained at chance throughout the trial (Figure 2A, right). Auditory learning therefore transfers an outcome-related code, not a cue-specific one.

The behavior decoder relied predominantly on hit-preferring cells: their absolute weights at 1.0 s after cue onset exceeded those of miss-preferring cells across all pooled cells and in 9 of 10 mice individually (Figure 2B and inset), and hit-preferring cells also outnumbered miss-preferring cells in the pooled population (2520 vs 2038; Figure 2C). Within the first transfer light-water block, hit-preferring cells evaluated on hit trials were more trial-stable than miss-preferring cells on miss trials (Figure 2D), and at cue +1 s they also showed higher mean activity on hit trials than miss-preferring cells did on miss trials, although this difference did not reach significance (Figure 2E). Auditory learning thus shapes a subset of outcome-selective, trial-stable hit cells — an encoding ensemble — whose activity tracks the outcome of the ongoing light trial.

To test whether this ensemble is causally required for transfer, we permanently tagged the cells active during the last audio-water training block with cFos-TRAP and silenced them during subsequent light training (Figure 2F, G). Silencing the tagged ensemble impaired light-water learning relative to control mice (Figure 2H), showing that the ensemble established by auditory learning is re-used for learning the new cue rather than merely correlated with it.

# Stable encoding ensemble fades over time

The encoding ensemble is causally required for immediate transfer (Figure 2). We next asked how long this ensemble remains available for the new task. In a separate cohort we inserted a 7-day home-cage interval, without training or imaging, between the last audio-water training block and the first light-water block (Figure 3A). The interval impaired light-water learning: mice with the 7-day gap attained lower hit rates across the first seven light-water blocks than continuously trained mice (Figure 3B; group effect of a two-way ANOVA over blocks 1–7, p = 0.023), and the deficit was already present in the very first block (Figure 3B inset; 0.52 ± 0.08 vs 0.13 ± 0.06, rank-sum p = 0.0045). Consistently, the population response of the first transfer block was less trial-stable after the gap: inter-trial divergence at cue +1 s tended to be higher than in the no-gap cohort (Figure 3C; 3.46 ± 0.22 vs 4.39 ± 0.33, rank-sum p = 0.056).

To ask whether the encoding ensemble itself survived the interval, we trained a cue-response decoder that discriminates the mean population activity of the 1 s before cue onset from that of the 1 s after cue onset in learned audio-water blocks, and applied it, without retraining, to the held-out transfer light-water block (Figure 3D). Because this decoder needs no outcome labels, every mouse of both cohorts could be included. Self-test by 5-fold cross-validation, in which one fifth of the trials (with their pre- and post-cue samples together) was held out of training in each fold, confirmed that both cohorts carried a robust cue-evoked code in the training context (out-of-fold accuracy 0.92 ± 0.03 and 0.96 ± 0.03; Figure 3D, left). In the no-gap cohort this code partially transferred: read out on the transfer block, the decoder exceeded the label-shuffled null at 2 of 16 time points and judged transfer trials as post-cue with probability 0.65 ± 0.04 (Figure 3D, top right). After 7 days it did not: the same decoder stayed within the null band at every time point (0 of 16) and its post-cue probability fell to 0.53 ± 0.04 (one-sided permutation p = 0.037 between cohorts; Figure 3D, bottom right). The cue-evoked ensemble therefore decays below the level that the new task can read out within 7 days.

Direct inspection of the ensemble confirmed this decay (Figure 3E). Among cells active at cue +1 s in learned audio-water blocks, the no-gap cohort re-engaged a substantial fraction during transfer light-water, whereas the 7-day gap cohort showed only sparse reactivation (Figure 3E; 511 and 290 learned-active cells, respectively). Quantitatively, the reactivation probability P(transfer-active | learned-active) at cue +1 s dropped from 0.15 ± 0.04 (n = 11 mice) to 0.018 ± 0.008 (n = 6 mice; rank-sum p = 0.011; Figure 3F). The encoding ensemble that supports immediate transfer thus fades over a 7-day interval, together with the behavioral advantage it confers.
