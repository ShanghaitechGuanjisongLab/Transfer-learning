# Introduction

Transfer of learning describes the influence of previously acquired knowledge and skills on the acquisition of new ones (Thorndike and Woodworth, 1901). The phenomenon pervades adaptive behavior, and its reach has long been taxonomized from near to far transfer (Barnett and Ceci, 2002). Prior frameworks have characterized transfer mainly at the behavioral and psychological levels (Perkins and Salomon, 1994), and even contemporary neural evidence on transfer itself has remained largely at brain-region resolution (Rasooli et al., 2024). A persistent conceptual difficulty is that the overall transfer advantage conflates at least two distinct processes: the ability to start a new task from an elevated initial state, and the ability to keep improving faster once the new task is underway. Whether and how these two components are supported by different cellular and circuit mechanisms is largely unknown.

Recent work has begun to approach this question at the level of neural populations. Mice can bidirectionally generalize sensorimotor task rules between touch and vision through abstract cortical representations, and silencing experiments indicate that congruent multimodal representations in associative cortex support such cross-modal generalization (Guyoton et al., 2025). At the representational level, cross-context decoder generalization has been used to define abstract coding geometry in hippocampus and prefrontal cortex (Bernardi et al., 2020). These studies establish that shared, abstract population codes can underlie rule transfer, but they leave open how previously recruited cell ensembles are reused at the onset of a new task, and how population organization evolves over subsequent training days.

Two long-standing literatures provide candidate mechanisms for these processes. In the engram literature, specific sparsely distributed cell assemblies are recruited during learning, and their tagging, inhibition, and reactivation causally control memory retrieval (Tonegawa et al., 2015), a framework consolidated in current reviews of engram cells (Josselyn and Tonegawa, 2020). Engrams of temporally proximate events tend to overlap through excitability-based competition (Rashid et al., 2016), providing a cellular substrate for linking related experiences (Choucry et al., 2024). The extent to which such ensemble reuse drives transfer of a sensorimotor association to a cue of a different modality, however, has not been tested directly. In the population-dynamics literature, behavior is generated within low-dimensional neural manifolds whose structure constrains what can be learned (Sadtler et al., 2014), and population dynamics more generally serve as the computational substrate of behavior (Vyas et al., 2020). Short-timescale learning can proceed by reassociating population activity within the existing manifold (Golub et al., 2018), and changes within a preserved population structure can likewise enable rapid learning (Perich et al., 2018), while manifold stability across months maintains consistent behavior (Gallego et al., 2020). Learning in motor cortex refines population structure at fine spatial scales rather than uniformly elevating activity (Komiyama et al., 2010); its structural counterpart is the rapid formation and selective stabilization of motor-learning synapses (Xu et al., 2009), and cross-day imaging shows that learning also amplifies sensory representations in visual cortex (Poort et al., 2015). In addition, stimulus-driven engagement of cortex is accompanied by a widespread reduction of trial-to-trial variability (Churchland et al., 2010). These findings suggest that prior training could leave behind a reusable population geometry that lowers trial-to-trial divergence at the onset of transfer and shapes how quickly the new task is subsequently learned.

A key open question is whether the initial advantage at transfer onset and the later advantage in learning speed rest on the same or on dissociable cellular signatures and circuits. Computational work provides one specific hypothesis for the latter: heterogeneity in intrinsic neuronal properties makes spiking network learning faster, more stable, and more robust in temporally structured tasks (Perez-Nieves et al., 2021). If response heterogeneity plays an analogous role in vivo, it should be elevated during transfer learning and should predict the speed of subsequent improvement, ideally with an identifiable upstream modulator. The motor thalamus is a plausible candidate, because motor learning refines thalamic influence on primary motor cortex to enable reliable execution of learned movements (Ramot et al., 2025); moreover, higher-order thalamocortical motifs are positioned to modulate cortical processing in a state-dependent manner (Halassa and Sherman, 2019), within cortico-thalamo-cortical loops whose cellular composition supports flexible top-down control (Shepherd and Yamawaki, 2021).

Here we addressed these questions by longitudinally imaging the same neurons across days in the tongue-related region of mouse primary motor cortex (MOp) during a cue-delayed-licking paradigm, in which mice first learned an auditory-cued water task and were then transferred to a visually cued version of the same task. This approach builds on our laboratory's earlier in vivo imaging of immediate early gene expression, which revealed layer-specific, sparsely coded long-term memory engram cells in the mammalian neocortex (Xie et al., 2014). MOp sits on the cue-to-lick sensorimotor pathway delineated by cortical activity flow in mice (Guo et al., 2014), and mouse motor-cortical populations exhibit low-dimensional, monotonic preparatory dynamics well suited to state-space analysis (Inagaki et al., 2018). Its output neurons are organized into projection classes with layer-specific functions (Oswald et al., 2013), and movement-specific signaling is distributed differentially across these classes (Currie et al., 2022), enabling a layered analysis of transfer. Combining across-day two-photon imaging in layers 2/3 and 5 with chemogenetic inhibition of tagged ensembles and of posterior thalamus, we decomposed the cross-modal transfer advantage into an initial-performance component tied to reactivation of prior ensembles and reduced trial-to-trial divergence, and a subsequent-learning component tied to enhanced response heterogeneity under thalamic modulation. Critically, the two components proved doubly dissociable: inhibiting the tagged ensemble impairs initial transfer performance, whereas inhibiting the posterior thalamus flattens the learning slope while leaving initial performance intact. A one-week pause between tasks abolishes the reuse of prior ensembles and attenuates the transfer advantage, whereas a network simulation reproduces how inhibition inherited from prior training raises response heterogeneity, suppresses noise-triggered reverse learning, and thereby accelerates transfer. Encoding and decoding analyses further characterize how cue and choice information is carried by MOp populations during transfer (Runyan et al., 2017), and how this relates to the reuse of prior-task cells. Together, these results show that the initial and learning-speed advantages of transfer rest on dissociable cellular signatures and circuits — ensemble reuse setting where transfer starts, and thalamus-modulated response heterogeneity determining how fast it proceeds — offering a cell-ensemble-level account of how prior experience shapes new learning.

# Highlights
- Studies of transfer itself have remained at behavioral, psychological, and brain-region levels of resolution, leaving its mechanism unresolved at the level of cell ensembles.
- Adjacent population-level work has crossed this resolution limit but not the object: shared abstract population codes support cross-modal generalization, yet how prior-task ensembles are reused during transfer is unknown.
- Engram studies establish that specific cell assemblies are tagged, reactivated, and causally control memory retrieval, but whether such ensemble reuse transfers a sensorimotor association across cue modalities remains untested.
- Population-dynamics studies show that learning reorganizes activity within constrained low-dimensional manifolds with preserved structure and reduced trial-to-trial variability, but have not examined how a manifold left by one task shapes acquisition of another.
- Computational models suggest that neuronal heterogeneity accelerates learning in temporally structured tasks, yet this prediction, and thalamocortical circuits as its upstream modulator, await in vivo verification in transfer learning.
